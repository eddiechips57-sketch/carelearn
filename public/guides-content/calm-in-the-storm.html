Introduction • Curated Action Guide

You Are Not Alone in the Storm

If you are reading this, chances are you have recently found yourself standing in the middle of an emotional "storm."

Perhaps it was your child, overwhelmed by harsh lights and noise in a busy supermarket, suddenly dropping to the floor screaming and unable to be comforted. Perhaps it was an elderly parent with dementia throwing a plate of food in sudden fear, or an adult you support on shift pacing the hallway, slamming doors, and shouting.

In those high-tension moments, your heart races. You might feel judged by strangers, physically exhausted, and deeply anxious about what to do next. It is all too easy to feel like you are failing.

Here is the single most important truth in care: All behaviour is communication.

When an individual—whether a young child, a neurodivergent teenager, or an older adult living with cognitive decline—experiences a meltdown, they are not trying to "be bad" or manipulate you. Their internal circuit breaker has tripped.

This playbook isn't about turning you into a clinical psychologist or forcing you to read a 300-page textbook. Instead, we have curated the gold-standard clinical evidence—including NICE Guidelines (NG11 & NG97), Professor Andrew McDonnell's Low Arousal Approach, and frontline guidance from the National Autistic Society and Alzheimer's Society—into plain-English steps, visual guides, and ready-to-print checklists you can use tonight.

Chapter 1

The Anatomy of a Meltdown: What Is Actually Happening?

To help someone calm down, we first need to understand what is happening inside their brain and nervous system. Many people confuse a tantrum with a meltdown, but treating a meltdown like a tantrum is the fastest way to make a crisis explode.

Tantrum (Goal-Directed) Meltdown (Nervous System Overload)
Cause: Frustration over not getting a desired item, activity, or outcome. Cause: Total sensory, emotional, physical, or cognitive overload.
Brain State: The thinking brain is still active; the person often checks to see if you are watching. Brain State: Survival mode (Fight, Flight, or Freeze). The thinking brain is completely offline.
Resolution: Stops quickly if they get what they want or realize the boundary is firm. Resolution: Cannot be stopped by rewards or threats; requires safety, quiet, and time to pass.

When a meltdown begins, the brain's logical control centre (the prefrontal cortex) shuts down, and the emergency alarm centre (the amygdala) takes the wheel. Because their brain believes they are in danger, they physically cannot process lectures, logic, or warnings.

Chapter 2 • Spotting Early Warning Signs Page 4
Chapter 2

The "Anxious Orange" Stage: Catching the Rumble Early

Almost every meltdown gives off early warning sparks before catching fire. If we learn to spot a person's unique Anxious Orange signals, we can gently step in and prevent 80% of meltdowns before they reach crisis point.

Physical Signals

Tense raised shoulders, clenched fists or jaw, pacing back and forth, foot-tapping, flushed cheeks, or sudden shallow breathing.

Behavioural Signals

Increased self-soothing (rocking, hand-flapping), asking the exact same question repeatedly, hiding under blankets, or refusing simple requests.

Sensory Signals

Covering ears with hands, squinting at indoor lights, pulling at collars or socks, or pushing away plates and textures.

Vocal Signals

High-pitched or rapid speech, humming loudly to block out noise, swearing, or suddenly going completely silent and shutting down.

Your 3-Step "Orange Stage" Rescue Plan

  1. 1. Turn Down the Sensory Volume: Immediately switch off background TVs/radios, dim bright overhead lights, or step out of crowded rooms.
  2. 2. Use Distraction & Redirection: Don't mention their behaviour. Instead, casually offer a regulating shift: "I'm going to grab a cold drink from the kitchen, want to come help me choose?"
  3. 3. Drop the Current Demand: If a worksheet, bath, or chore is causing the rumble, pause it immediately. You can always come back to it later.

Visual Guide: Agitating Posture vs. The "Calm Anchor" Stance

What NOT To Do: The Agitator

  • • Standing square-on & leaning over: Triggers the brain's threat reflex.
  • • Crossed arms or pointing fingers: Looks confrontational and authoritarian.
  • • Standing too close (inside 1 metre): Makes a distressed person feel cornered and trapped.
  • • Demanding eye contact: Increases sensory panic for neurodivergent and distressed individuals.

What TO Do: The Calm Anchor

  • • Turn sideways (45-degree angle): Instantly removes the feeling of a head-on standoff.
  • • Open, relaxed hands at your sides: Shows you are safe and non-threatening.
  • • One-arm's length + distance: Gives breathing room and a clear path to the door.
  • • Lower your height: Sit in a chair or kneel at a safe distance so you are below eye level.
Chapter 3 • Co-Regulation & Self-Control Page 5
Chapter 3

Mastering Your Mind: Why Your Calm Is Their Lifeline

Before you can calm someone else down, you have to calm your own nervous system. Humans have "mirror neurons" in their brains that automatically copy the emotional state of the people around them. If you walk into a crisis with a sharp voice, racing heart, and panicked energy, the distressed person's brain will read that as proof that there is an emergency.

Sharing your steadiness with someone who has lost theirs is called co-regulation. Even when you feel shaken on the inside, you can project "Calm Anchor" signals on the outside using this 15-second reset:

Step 1: Check-In

Notice Your Adrenaline

Notice if your jaw is clenched or if you're worrying about people staring. Acknowledge it: "My adrenaline is spiking, and that's normal."

Step 2: Release

Drop Your Shoulders

Physically drop your shoulders two inches away from your ears, unclench your hands, and take one slow exhale longer than your inhale.

Step 3: Anchor Mantra

Shift Your Goal

Repeat silently: "I don't need to win an argument right now. I only need to be the calmest person in this room."

On-the-Spot Carer Tool

The "Box & Sigh" Carer Reset Breath

You can do this silently while standing next to someone in distress. Bonus: when you breathe loudly and slowly enough for them to hear, they will often unconsciously start matching your breathing rhythm without you saying a word.

  • • Inhale (4 Seconds): Breathe in quietly through your nose.
  • • Hold (2 Seconds): Pause gently and drop your shoulders.
  • • Slow Sigh Out (6 Seconds): Exhale slowly through pursed lips.
Chapter 4 • Crisis De-escalation Page 6
Chapter 4

In the Eye of the Storm: What to Say & Do During a Crisis

When a person tips into the Red Crisis Zone, your game plan changes completely. You are no longer trying to teach manners, finish shopping, or get them dressed. Your only priority is emotional and physical safety.

1

Follow the "5-Word Rule" (Talk Less)

During a meltdown, spoken language sounds like loud static noise. Use five words or fewer at a time, speak slowly in a low pitch, and pause for 30 seconds between sentences.

2

Clear the Audience, Not Always the Person

Trying to physically drag or shepherd someone out of a room often triggers defensive hitting. Unless they are in immediate danger, quietly move other people and breakable objects out of the room instead.

3

Never Block the Exit

Standing in a doorway makes an overloaded brain feel caged. Step to the side so both you and the person have a safe route out.

4

Validate the Feeling, Not the Behaviour

You don't have to agree with throwing a plate to acknowledge their distress. Saying "I can see you are really overwhelmed" lowers defences immediately.

Exact Verbal Swap Scripts for High-Stress Moments

Avoid Saying This (Adds Fuel) Say This Instead (Builds Safety)
"Calm down! There is nothing to be upset about!" "You are safe. I am right here."
"Tell me what's wrong! Use your words!" "We don't have to talk right now. Take your time."
"If you don't stop shouting, you're losing your iPad." "It's too noisy in here. Let's sit quietly."
"Look at me when I'm talking to you!" (Stay quiet, place a glass of water nearby, and wait.)
Chapter 5 • The Post-Crisis Recovery Phase Page 7
Chapter 5

The Recovery Phase: Avoiding the "Second Wave"

Once the shouting stops or the tears begin to slow down, it is natural to breathe a sigh of relief and assume the meltdown is over. Be very careful here.

This fragile window is the Blue Recovery Zone. The person has survived an adrenaline crash. Their emotional battery is at zero, and they often feel shaky, embarrassed, thirsty, or exhausted. If we rush in too fast with questions ("Why did you do that?"), demands for an apology, or instructions to clean up the mess right away, their nervous system will spike straight back into a Second Wave Meltdown.

Step 1 • First 10 Minutes

Silent Comfort & Hydration

Crying and shouting dehydrate the body rapidly. Place a cup of water or juice, tissues, or a favourite blanket within reach without asking any questions.

Step 2 • Mins 10 to 45

Low-Demand Rest ("Safe Nest")

Allow them to sleep, listen to familiar music, watch a comfort show, or sit in a dimly lit room. Their brain needs 30–60 minutes for stress hormones to clear.

Step 3 • Rebuilding Trust

Warm Reconnection First

Reconnect with a warm, neutral sentence that shows you aren't holding a grudge: "I'm glad you're feeling a bit better. Would you like some toast?"

Step 4 • Looking After You

The Carer's Physical Shake-Off

Once they are safe, step into the bathroom or kitchen, splash cold water on your wrists, shake out your hands, and drink a glass of water yourself.

When Should You Talk About What Happened?

If the person has the cognitive ability to reflect (such as an older child or teen), wait at least several hours or until the next day when everyone is back in the "Calm Green" zone. Keep it collaborative rather than punishing: "Yesterday got really loud in the shop. Next time it feels too bright, what secret signal can you give me so we can leave early?"

4 Real-World Playbooks: Step-by-Step Action Plans

Scenario 1 • Public & Sensory Overload For Parents & Guardians

The Supermarket Floor Meltdown

Situation: Harsh strip lighting and aisle noise trigger your child to cover their ears, drop to the floor, and scream while strangers stare.

1. Ignore onlookers completely: Strangers' opinions do not matter. Turn your back to the aisle to create a privacy shield around your child.

2. Crouch & Cut Sensory Input: Kneel two steps away. Offer ear defenders, pull up their hoodie, or pop sunglasses on them. Say softly: "Too loud. I've got you."

3. Exit First, Shop Later: Leave the trolley with a staff member and move to the quiet car or outside bench until their breathing slows.

Scenario 2 • Dementia & Personal Care For Family Carers & Support Workers

Refusing a Wash, Shower, or Clothing Change

Situation: An elderly relative or resident shouts "Get out! Leave me alone!" and swats your hands away when you try to help them wash.

1. Stop & Back Off Warmly: Never tug clothing while someone is distressed—it feels like an assault to a confused brain. Step back with open palms: "No problem at all, we'll leave it for now."

2. The 20-Minute Warm Reset: Leave the room. Pre-warm the bathroom, warm a towel on the radiator, and bring a cup of tea first.

3. Offer Choice & Dignity: Return later without saying the word "shower." Try: "Here is a warm flannel for your hands while we chat," and keep them covered with a towel as much as possible.

Scenario 3 • Transitions & Screens For Home & Supported Living

Explosive Refusal When Ending Screen Time or Leaving

Situation: It is time to turn off a tablet/TV or leave for an appointment, and the person yells "No!" and throws the controller.

1. Never Snatch the Device or Cut Wi-Fi Cold: Abrupt endings feel like a cliff-edge drop in dopamine. Let the current video/round finish or use a visual sand timer 5 minutes beforehand.

2. "Join Before You Redirect": Spend 60 seconds asking about their game/show first: "Show me what you're building before we pause it."

3. Use "First / Then" Choices: "First tablet on the charging dock, then we pick car music. Do you want your red hoodie or blue coat?"

Scenario 4 • High Agitation Indoors For Frontline Workers & Families

Pacing, Slamming Doors, and Sweeping Objects

Situation: A teenager or adult client is pacing rapidly, shouting threats, and knocks a mug off the table.

1. Leave the Mess on the Floor: Do not bend down near their feet to pick up broken items while they are still raging. Keep two arm's lengths away near the exit.

2. Open an Escape Valve: Point toward a low-stimulation space: "I can see you're really angry. The back garden door is open if you need air. I'll wait in the hall."

Chapter 7 • Validated Audience Questions

The Hard Questions: What Carers Ask Most

When families and support workers post in online care communities late at night, these are the six acute questions they ask most—answered using safe, low-arousal clinical protocols.

1. "If I drop my demand during a meltdown, aren't I 'rewarding' bad behaviour?"

No. Clinical research (NICE NG11 / Studio 3) shows that you cannot teach a lesson when the prefrontal cortex is offline. Dropping a demand during a Red Crisis is like throwing a lifebuoy to someone drowning—you don't teach swimming lessons while they are going under. You teach skills and boundaries later when they are in the Calm Green Zone.

2. "What if they try to hit, bite, or grab my hair/clothing?"

Distance is your primary shield. Never try to restrain or hold someone down unless trained and there is an immediate life-threatening risk (restraint massively spikes panic and injury risk). Keep two arm's lengths away, use a soft cushion or pillow held in front of your torso as a non-aggressive buffer if retreating, and if hair or clothing is grabbed, move your hands inward toward their knuckles and gently stabilise rather than pulling back (pulling back tightens their grip reflex).

3. "How do I protect younger siblings or other residents in the same room?"

Always move the audience, not the distressed person. Attempting to physically march an overloaded person out of a room usually triggers violence. Have a pre-agreed code phrase for siblings or co-workers (e.g., "Time for kitchen movie club") so others calmly exit to a safe room while one calm adult remains near the doorway.

4. "What if an adult with dementia or autism suddenly becomes aggressive out of nowhere?"

According to NICE Guideline NG97 (Dementia), sudden unexplained aggression in someone who struggles to communicate is most commonly caused by undiagnosed physical pain, a Urinary Tract Infection (UTI), severe constipation, or dehydration. Always arrange a prompt GP/medical check to rule out infection or pain before assuming it is purely behavioural.

5. "How do I handle strangers staring or making rude comments in public?"

Keep a small laminated Medical/Autism/Dementia Awareness Card in your pocket to silently hand to a store employee or bystander so you never have to break your calm focus to explain yourself aloud. Remember: a stranger's discomfort lasts 30 seconds; your loved one's sense of safety with you lasts a lifetime.

6. "When is a crisis too big to handle alone, and who should I call?"

If anyone is at immediate risk of serious physical harm, lock yourself and others in a safe room and call emergency services (999 in the UK / 911 in the US). Clearly state: "I am calling about a mental health / autism / dementia sensory crisis, not a crime. Please send officers or paramedics trained in low-arousal de-escalation without sirens if possible." For non-life-threatening urgent distress in the UK, call NHS 111 (select Option 2 for Mental Health Crisis).

Printable Toolkit #2 • Spot Hidden Triggers

ABC Behaviour & Trigger Pattern Log

carelearn.site

Why use an ABC chart? Meltdowns often feel "out of nowhere" until you write down three or four in a row. Suddenly you spot the pattern—like every meltdown happening at 4:30 PM when blood sugar drops, or right after noisy shift handovers.

A = Antecedent (Before) What was happening right before? Where were they? Who was there? Noise, hunger, pain, or a task demand?
B = Behaviour (During) What did you actually see and hear? Describe actions plainly (e.g., "screamed for 10 mins, threw cup").
C = Consequence & Calmer (After) How did people respond, and what eventually helped the person settle down?
Date, Time & Setting A — What Happened Right Before? (Triggers/Sensory/Needs) B — What Did the Behaviour Look Like? C — What Helped Them Calm Down?
Example:
Tue 4:15 PM
Kitchen		 Radio was on loud, hadn't eaten since 12pm, asked to put shoes on. Covered ears, shouted "No!", threw shoe at door. Turned radio off, backed away, offered banana & 15 mins quiet time.
