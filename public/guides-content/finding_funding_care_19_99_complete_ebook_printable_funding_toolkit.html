Introduction • Navigating the Social Care Maze

Why Finding and Funding Care Feels Like Walking Through Fog

When an ageing parent or relative reaches the point where they can no longer manage safely at home alone, families are usually thrust into a bewildering administrative maze overnight. Suddenly you are forced to learn unfamiliar terms like means tests, capital thresholds, continuing healthcare, reablement, and deferred payment agreements—often while dealing with emotional exhaustion and grief.

Many families make costly mistakes simply because they don't know their statutory rights under the law. They assume they must sell their parents' home immediately, or they skip asking the local council for a formal Care Needs Assessment because they assume they have too much savings.

The Golden Rule of Care Funding: Everyone has a legal right to a Care Needs Assessment from their local authority, regardless of their financial savings or property value.

Never let a local authority skip your care needs assessment just because they assume you will be a "self-funder." An official assessment establishes your baseline needs and opens doors to NHS funding and council advice.

This playbook translates official social care standards—including the Care Act 2014 Statutory Guidance ↗, Age UK Care Funding Guides ↗, and Independent Age Social Care Portals ↗—into a clear, plain-English roadmap.

Chapter 1

The Care Needs Assessment: Your Legal Right (And How to Ace It)

Under Section 9 of the **Care Act 2014**, every local authority in England and Wales has a legal duty to carry out a free **Care Needs Assessment** for anyone who appears to have care and support needs. This assessment evaluates how well the person can manage everyday tasks—such as washing, dressing, preparing meals, and moving around their home safely—independent of what is in their bank account.

Visual Blueprint: The 4-Step Social Services Assessment Journey

🚨 Describe Their "Worst Day" Older adults often put on a brave face when an official visits: "Oh I'm fine, I manage!" Always describe how they function on their absolute worst, most exhausted day, not their best morning.
👥 Be Present at the Visit Never let social services assess a frail parent alone. Sit in to ensure safety issues (like recent falls or missed pills) are fully documented.
📋 Request a Carer's Assessment Too If you provide unpaid care, you have a separate legal right to your own **Carer's Assessment** to get respite support or direct payments for yourself.

🏡 Does the House Get Counted? If moving into a care home, your property is usually counted as capital—unless a spouse, civil partner, or relative aged 60+ (or disabled) still lives there. For home care (domiciliary care), your home is **never** counted.
⚠️ Beware "Deliberate Deprivation" Gifting your house or large savings to your children right before needing care to avoid fees is illegal ("deprivation of assets"). Councils investigate financial records back several years and can reverse the transfer.

Comparing Care Settings: Domiciliary Care, Care Homes & Nursing Homes

Choosing where care takes place is one of the most emotional decisions a family makes. Understanding the differences between visiting home care, residential homes, and 24-hour nursing homes ensures you match the environment to their actual medical needs.

Visual Blueprint: The 3 Primary Care Delivery Settings

🏡 Live-In Care Alternative For those who refuse care homes, full-time live-in carers provide 1-to-1 round-the-clock support in their own home for comparable monthly costs to residential care.
⭐ Check CQC Ratings Always check the Care Quality Commission (CQC) inspection rating (*Outstanding, Good, Requires Improvement, Inadequate*) before shortlisting any home.
🍲 Unannounced Visits When touring care homes, drop in unannounced on a Tuesday afternoon to observe dining quality, staff warmth, and ambient noise levels.

Chapter 4

Unlocking Free NHS Continuing Healthcare (CHC) Funding

Many families spend thousands of pounds on care home fees without realizing their relative's primary needs are actually **health-based**, not social care-based. Under **NHS Continuing Healthcare (CHC)**, if someone has a "primary health need" (such as end-of-life care, severe neurological impairment, or complex medical wounds), the NHS is legally required to **fund 100% of their care fees** in any setting—including care homes or at home—regardless of savings or property (NHS CHC Guidelines ↗).

Visual Blueprint: The 12 Domains of the NHS CHC Checklist

📋 Request Checklist Ask your GP, hospital discharge team, or social worker explicitly: "We would like an NHS Continuing Healthcare Checklist assessment conducted."
⚡ Fast-Track Pathway If your loved one has a rapidly deteriorating condition or end-of-life prognosis, ask for the **Fast-Track CHC Pathway** which bypasses panels and secures funding in 48 hours.
💡 Funded Nursing Care (FNC) If they don't qualify for full CHC but live in a nursing home requiring a registered nurse, the NHS pays a flat weekly **FNC contribution** directly to the home.

Chapter 6

4 Real-World Playbooks: Means Tests, House Sales & CHC Appeals

Playbook 1: Council Refuses Care Needs Assessment If social services say "Your relative has too much savings for an assessment," politely quote the Care Act 2014: Every person has a legal right to a Care Needs Assessment regardless of their financial position. Insist in writing that the assessment be booked.
Playbook 2: Pressured to Sell House Immediately If a care home insists the house must be sold within weeks to pay fees, request a **Deferred Payment Agreement (DPA)** from your local council. The council loans the care fees using the house as security so you can sell at market value later.
Playbook 3: NHS CHC Application Rejected If your CHC funding application is turned down, do not accept the first decision. Request a full written breakdown, gather supporting medical letters from consultants, and invoke your right to an **NHS Independent Review Panel**.
Playbook 4: Care Home Top-Up Fees Demand If a council-funded care home placement demands extra weekly "third-party top-up fees" from family, ensure you sign a formal agreement and check that the top-up is genuinely voluntary and sustainable long-term.

Chapter 7 • Validated Carer Questions

Finding & Funding Care: The Hard Questions Answered

Here are the six most urgent questions families ask about paying for care and protecting their parents' assets:

1. "Can we legally give our parents' house to the children to avoid care home fees?"

No. Under UK social care law, this is classed as "deliberate deprivation of assets." Councils check financial history and can treat the house as still belonging to your parent during means-testing—leaving you owing back-fees while your parent has no legal claim to the property.

2. "What happens when savings drop below the £23,250 upper capital limit?"

The moment savings drop to around £24,000, contact your local council immediately to request a financial reassessment. The council will step in to fund the difference once savings cross the £23,250 threshold downward.

3. "Does Attendance Allowance stop if someone moves into a care home?"

If your relative is self-funding their care home place entirely out of their own pocket, **Attendance Allowance continues**. However, if the local council is subsidising their place, Attendance Allowance stops after 28 days.

4. "How do we choose between a cheap care home and an expensive one?"

Look beyond the lobby decor. Check CQC inspection reports for safety and leadership ratings, talk to existing residents' families during visits, and observe staff interaction quality during lunch service.

5. "Can the NHS refuse CHC funding even if someone has severe dementia?"

Yes. Dementia alone does not automatically grant CHC funding unless the symptoms (such as aggressive behavioural distress, swallowing risks, or medical vulnerability) reach a high level of complexity and intensity.

6. "Where can I get free, accredited financial advice on paying for care?"

Contact **Age UK Advice (0800 678 1602)**, **Independent Age (0800 319 6789)**, or the **Society of Later Life Advisers (SOLLA)** for expert, regulated guidance.
