Introduction • Curated Action Guide

When Words Aren't Working, Everything Feels Harder

Imagine waking up in a foreign country with a splitting toothache, or feeling terrified because someone is leading you into a noisy car, and you have no way to ask "Where are we going?" or say "My mouth hurts." When you try to point or pull away, people just speak louder at you and usher you along.

Within ten minutes, you would either scream, push someone away, or shut down completely.

This is the daily reality for millions of non-speaking or minimally speaking children, neurodivergent teenagers, and adults living with learning disabilities, stroke-related aphasia, or advanced dementia. And for the parents, family guardians, and support workers who love and care for them, the constant guessing game—"Are they hungry? In pain? Bored? Anxious?"—is emotionally exhausting.

Communication is not just speech. Speech is only one tool in a much bigger toolbox.

Even when someone speaks fluently most of the day, high anxiety or sensory overload can cause "situational mutism" or temporary speech loss. Having visual and non-verbal backups helps everyone.

In this playbook, we have distilled clinical speech and language frameworks from the Royal College of Speech and Language Therapists (RCSLT) ↗, NICE Guidelines (CG170 & NG11) ↗, The Makaton Charity ↗, and PECS UK ↗ into simple, zero-cost tools you can start using at home or on shift today.

Chapter 1

The "Total Communication" Pyramid: How to Meet Them Where They Are

One of the biggest traps families and new care workers fall into is thinking: "If I use picture cards or objects, they will get lazy and never try to talk." **Decades of clinical research prove the exact opposite.** Picture cards, gestures, and real objects remove panic and build the brain pathways that actually *encourage* vocal sounds and spoken words.

Speech and Language Therapists use the **Hierarchy of Symbolic Development**. Spoken words sit right at the very top—they are the most abstract and hardest to process. When someone is struggling to understand you, **drop down the pyramid** to a more concrete level:

Visual Guide: The 5-Level Communication Pyramid

When spoken words fail, step down to a lower, clearer layer of the pyramid.

💡 The Secret Power of "Objects of Reference" (Level 2)

Watch Sense UK Objects of Reference Guide ↗

For young children, individuals with profound learning disabilities, or adults with advanced dementia who no longer recognize 2D pictures, real physical objects work like magic. Consistently hand them the exact same object right before an activity:

🥄 Wooden Spoon = Dinner time
🧼 Soft Flannel = Wash / Bath time
🔑 Car Key Fob = Going in the car
🧸 Small Cushion = Quiet rest time

Chapter 2

Becoming a Communication Detective: Sounds, Actions & Echolalia

Every person who doesn't use traditional speech already has their own rich language—we just need to learn their dictionary. Instead of viewing repetitive actions or vocal sounds as "random behaviours," ask yourself: "What job is this action doing for them right now?" (Learn more about Gestalt Language Processing & Echolalia here ↗).

What You See or Hear What It Often Means How You Should Respond
Hand-Leading (Pulling your hand toward the fridge or door) Using your hand as a tool to request something specific they can't reach or open. Walk with them, point to the two likely items, and model: "Juice or apple? Juice!"
Echolalia (Repeating a line from a cartoon, advert, or your last word) Gestalt Processing: They tie a whole phrase to the feeling they had when they first heard it. Look at the emotion behind the quote. A scary cartoon quote often means "I feel nervous."
Loud Humming, Monotone Moaning, or Ear-Covering Acoustic shielding—creating predictable vibration to block out overwhelming room noise. Never tell them to "shh." Lower environmental noise immediately and offer ear defenders.
Sudden Dropping to the Floor ("Flopping") "I don't know where we are going next, my legs are tired, or this transition feels unsafe." Pause, show a picture or object of the destination, and offer a 2-minute seated count.

The Golden Clinical Rule

The "10-Second Silent Wait" Rule

Neurodivergent individuals and adults with cognitive decline or brain injury often take **up to 10 full seconds** to process a spoken sentence. Most carers repeat the question after just 2 seconds—which resets their brain's processing queue back to zero!

Ask Once.

Then count slowly to 10 in your head while smiling warmly.

Chapter 3

The Magic of "First / Then" Boards & Visual Routines

Spoken words disappear into thin air the second you say them. If someone is anxious, distracted, or has working memory challenges, your spoken instruction is gone within two seconds. **A visual picture stays still.** They can look back at it as many times as they need to feel safe.

The single most effective tool in all of care and parenting is the **"First / Then" (or "Now / Next") Board**. It shows two simple boxes side by side:
• FIRST (Now): The necessary daily task (e.g., *Put on shoes*, *Take medicine*, *Wash hands*).
• THEN (Next): A motivating, comforting, or preferred activity right after (e.g., *Garden swing*, *Cup of tea*, *iPad time*).

Visual Blueprint: How a "First / Then" Board Works

Ends 75% of Transition Refusals
1. Tap, Don't Lecture Point to the two cards and say just four words: "First teeth, then video." If they protest, don't argue—simply tap the board gently again.
2. Let Them Remove It As soon as the "First" task is finished, let the person pull the card off or tick it as **"All Done."** That physical action releases a hit of achievement dopamine.
3. Always Honour "Then" In the beginning, deliver the "Then" activity immediately so they learn 100% trust that the board always keeps its promise. (Jump to Printable First/Then Cards on p. 11 ↘)

Chapter 4

How to Offer Choices Without Overwhelming Their Brain

When you cannot speak easily, other people control almost every minute of your day—what you wear, what you eat, when you bathe, and where you sit. That lack of control is one of the biggest hidden triggers for challenging behaviour. However, asking an open question like "What would you like to eat?" requires massive memory recall. Instead, use The Rule of Two Visual Choices:

Visual Technique: The 30cm "Two-Hand Choice" Method (Eye-Gaze, Pointing or Touch)

1. Hold Up Two Real Items Hold one option in your left hand and one in your right hand 30cm apart so you can clearly see which one their eyes or hand move toward.
2. Accept Gaze or Touch Never force them to "say the word" before handing it over. Model the word warmly as you give it: "You looked at banana! Here's banana."
3. Stop Quizzing Being tested all day ("What colour is this? Say please!") triggers anxiety. Switch to calm narration without demanding a performance.

Everyday Communication Swaps (High-Pressure vs. Low-Pressure)

❌ High-Pressure / Confusing ✅ Clear, Supportive & Visual
"What do you want for lunch today?" (Too open) (Hold up both items) "Soup or sandwich?"
"Say 'drink'! I won't give it to you until you say 'drink'!" (As they tap the cup) "Drink! Here is your cold drink."
"Don't run in the hallway!" (Brain only hears 'Run!') "Walking feet, please." (Walk slowly beside them)
"We're popping out in a bit to see the doctor then Nanna's." (Show First/Then card) "First Doctor, then Nanna's house."

Pain vs. Frustration: How to Spot Hidden Illness Without Words

According to NICE Guideline NG11 ↗ and the clinical Disability Distress Assessment Tool (DISDAT), undiagnosed physical pain is the #1 missed cause of sudden self-injury, head-banging, biting, or aggression in non-verbal children and adults. Because many neurodivergent individuals and people with dementia process internal body signals differently (interoception), they rarely point neatly to the sore spot.

Clinical Body Map: Where Non-Verbal Behaviour Signals Physical Pain

1. Dental / Ear / Sinus Pain

Hitting Cheek, Biting Wrist, or Ear-Pulling

Chewing hard on shirt collars, pushing knuckles into the jaw, or refusing crunchy/cold foods often signals a hidden tooth abscess or ear infection.

2. Acid Reflux / Stomach Pain

Arching Back, Throat-Clearing & Night Waking

Silent acid reflux burns more when lying flat. Watch for waking up screaming at 2 AM, leaning tummy over sofa arms, or tapping the chest.

3. "How do I teach 'No' or 'Stop' safely when they usually bite or hit to refuse?"

Someone hits or bites because it is the fastest way they know to make an unwanted demand go away. Give them a faster, easier tool: place a bright yellow **"Break / Not Now"** card next to them. The moment they even brush their hand against that card, immediately step back and say: "You asked for a break! Okay, we'll stop for two minutes."

4. "My child/client can talk in full sentences sometimes, so why do they go completely mute when upset?"

Spoken language requires huge amounts of energy from the brain's prefrontal cortex (Broca's area). During sensory overload, illness, or anxiety, the brain diverts energy to survival mode, causing **situational mutism** or intermittent speech loss. Always keep a low-tech visual choice board available—even for verbal individuals—for high-stress moments.

5. "Should we start with high-tech tablet apps or simple paper picture cards?"

Always keep **paper/laminated visuals** alongside any tablet app. Paper cards never run out of battery, can go in the bath or swimming pool, don't have distracting YouTube notifications, and can be stuck directly where they are needed (like on the fridge door or bathroom mirror).

6. "How do we get school staff, agency carers, or grandparents to actually use the visuals consistently?"

People skip complicated 30-page folders. Give them **Toolkit #2 (The 1-Page 'How I Communicate' Passport)** on Page 12 of this guide. It takes 60 seconds to read and lists the exact top 5 gestures/cues and 3 golden rules for anyone walking onto shift.
