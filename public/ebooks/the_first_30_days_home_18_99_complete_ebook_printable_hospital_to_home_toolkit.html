Introduction • Surviving the Transition Home

Closing the "Hospital Discharge Cliff-Edge"

In hospital, a team of nurses, doctors, occupational therapists, and healthcare assistants surround your loved one 24 hours a day. Then, often with less than 24 hours' notice, you get the phone call: "Good news—they are medically fit for discharge. Transport is bringing them home this afternoon."

Suddenly, you are standing in your living room holding a plastic bag stuffed with unfamiliar medication boxes, a crumpled discharge summary full of medical abbreviations, and a frail, exhausted relative who struggles to get to the toilet.

Clinical Fact: Nearly 1 in 5 older adults discharged from hospital are readmitted as an emergency within 30 days—and over half of those readmissions are preventable.

The culprits are almost never the original surgery or illness. They are **medication mix-ups, night-time bathroom falls, dehydration/UTIs, pressure sores, and carer exhaustion.**

This playbook translates official clinical transition standards—including NICE Guideline NG27 (Transition between hospital and home) ↗, NHS Discharge to Assess & Intermediate Care Guidance ↗, and Stroke Association Home Recovery Protocols ↗—into a clear, day-by-day plan anyone can follow.

Chapter 1

Before They Leave the Ward: How to Prevent an Unsafe Discharge

Hospitals are under immense pressure to free up beds. While being **"Medically Fit for Discharge" (MFFD)** means a doctor believes your loved one no longer needs acute hospital tests, it does **not** automatically mean it is safe for them to walk into an unequipped house alone at 7 PM on a Friday. Under NICE Guideline NG27 ↗ and Age UK Discharge Standards ↗, a discharge must be safe and coordinated.

Visual Guide: Understanding NHS "Discharge to Assess" Pathways & Your 6-Week Free Care Right

The 5 Non-Negotiables Before They Get in the Car or Ambulance

1. The Printed Discharge Summary & TTO Meds Never leave without a printed copy of the Discharge Letter (one copy goes to the GP, one stays in your home folder) and at least 7–14 days of **TTO ("To Take Out")** medications.
2. Occupational Therapy (OT) Equipment Delivered First If they cannot climb stairs or stand from a low toilet, ask the ward OT for a **commode, raised toilet seat, walking frame, or hospital bed** to be delivered *before* arrival.
3. Ask for "Intermediate Care / Reablement" (Free for up to 6 Weeks) In the UK, NHS/Council **Reablement Care** provides up to 6 weeks of free home care visits and physio after a hospital stay—regardless of savings—to help them regain independence.
4. District Nurse & Continence Referrals If they have surgical dressings, a catheter, pressure ulcer risk, or new incontinence, confirm the ward nurse has sent an active referral to the **Community District Nursing Team**.

Chapter 2

Setting Up the "Home Recovery Zone" (First 72 Hours)

Most home accidents happen in the first 72 hours because the house is still set up for a healthy person. Loose rugs, dim hallway landings, and low soft sofas become obstacle courses after surgery or a stroke. Use this **Recovery Room Blueprint** (adapted from Royal College of Occupational Therapists ↗ home adaptation guidance) to prepare their primary room in under an hour:

Architectural Blueprint: The Fall-Proof Downstairs Recovery Room Layout

💡 Banish Loose Rugs & Trailing Cables Roll up every scatter rug and bathmat immediately. Walking frames and post-stroke feet catch on a 5mm rug lip. Tape phone and lamp cables flat against skirting boards.
🔔 The £10 Wireless Doorbell Trick Shouting from another room doesn't work when someone is weak. Buy a cheap plug-in wireless doorbell—put the chime in your kitchen/bedroom and tape the push-button to their bedside table.

Chapter 3

Decoding the Hospital Medication Bag: Preventing Dangerous Double-Dosing

The #1 cause of emergency hospital readmission in the first 14 days is **medication error**. Why? Because hospitals often stop old blood pressure or blood-thinning tablets, start new ones, or supply generic packets that look completely different from the boxes still sitting in your kitchen cupboard at home. People accidentally take **both** the old cupboard box and the new hospital box—causing a dangerous double dose. (Follow NICE Guideline SC1 on Managing Medicines in Care ↗).

Visual Protocol: The Day-1 "Medication Reconciliation & Quarantine" System

Plain-English Decoder: What Those Hospital Abbreviations Actually Mean

Code on Box / Letter Plain-English Meaning Practical Spacing Rule
OD (Once Daily) Take **1 time** per day Give at the same time every day (check if morning e.g. water tablets, or night e.g. statins).
BD (Twice Daily) Take **2 times** per day Space roughly 10–12 hours apart (e.g., **8:00 AM** and **8:00 PM**).
TDS (Three Times Daily) Take **3 times** per day Morning (**8:00 AM**), Afternoon (**2:00 PM**), and Bedtime (**8:00 PM**).
QDS (Four Times Daily) Take **4 times** per day Space at least 4–6 hours apart (never give paracetamol closer than 4 hours apart!).
PRN (When Required) Only take **if needed** (e.g. pain/laxative) Do not give automatically if they have no pain or symptoms; always log exact time given.

Chapter 4 • Critical Physical Protection

Preventing Pressure Sores, Postural Drop Falls & Blood Clots

A painful **pressure ulcer (bed sore)** can begin developing in as little as **2 to 3 hours** if a frail person sits or lies in one position without shifting weight. Once skin breaks down, it can lead to life-threatening sepsis. Follow the NHS NICE CG179 / SSKIN Bundle Protocol ↗ every morning and evening:

Clinical Diagram: The 5 Pressure Ulcer Danger Zones & The 3-Second "Finger Blanch Test"

🦶 "Float the Heels" & Never Rub Red Skin Place a soft pillow lengthwise under their calves so their heels float slightly off the mattress. **Never vigorously rub red bony skin**—rubbing tears fragile underlying capillaries. Use barrier cream if incontinence is present.
⏳ The "Sit-for-60, Stand-for-10" Dizziness Rule After days in a hospital bed, blood pressure drops suddenly when standing up (*postural hypotension*). Always have them **sit on the edge of the bed for 60 seconds** pumping their ankles, then **stand still holding the frame for 10 seconds** before taking a single step.

Chapter 5

Caring After a Stroke or Hip/Joint Surgery: Protecting Vulnerable Limbs

When caring for someone after a stroke (who may have weakness on one side of their body, called *hemiparesis*) or after a hip replacement/fracture repair, a single incorrect pull on an arm or low seat angle can dislocate a joint. Follow these Stroke Association Limb Protection Guidelines ↗:

Visual Golden Rules: Protecting the Stroke Shoulder & The "Weak-In First, Strong-Out First" Dressing Secret

👁️ Watch for "Visual Neglect" After a stroke, some people stop noticing the left or right half of their plate or room. Rotate their dinner plate halfway through the meal so they see all the food.
🪑 The 90-Degree Hip Rule After hip surgery, never let them sit in a low chair where their knees are higher than their hips, cross their legs, or bend forward past 90 degrees to pick up slippers.
🔋 Respect "Neuro-Fatigue" Post-stroke fatigue is a biological brain exhaustion, not laziness. Limit visitors to 20 minutes and schedule a mandatory 90-minute quiet rest every afternoon.

Chapter 6

Spotting Silent Complications Early: Delirium, UTIs, Clots & Sepsis

In older adults, serious infections like a **Urinary Tract Infection (UTI)** or **chest infection** often do *not* cause a classic high fever first. Instead, the earliest warning sign is **sudden confusion, hallucinations, or uncharacteristic drowsiness (Delirium)**. Use this 3-tier clinical triage matrix so you never second-guess who to call:

Visual Triage Chart: Green (Home Monitor) vs. Amber (GP / NHS 111 Today) vs. Red (Dial 999)

Scenario A: "They had a fall on the carpet but say they aren't hurt." Never lift someone straight off the floor by their arms! Check for pain/bleeding first. If they take blood thinners (Apixaban, Rivaroxaban, Warfarin, Clopidogrel) and bumped their head, **always call 111/999** even if they feel fine.
Scenario B: "The promised care agency hasn't turned up on Day 1." Look at the top of the Discharge Summary for the **Hospital Discharge Hub / Integrated Care Team** phone number, or call your council's **Adult Social Care Emergency Duty Team** immediately.

Chapter 7 • Validated Carer Questions

Hospital Discharge & Stroke Recovery: The Hard Questions Answered

Here are the six most urgent questions families and domiciliary carers ask when a loved one comes home from hospital:

1. "Can the hospital force me to become a full-time unpaid carer if I work or have a bad back?"

No. Under the UK Care Act 2014 and NHS Discharge Guidance, family members have no legal obligation to provide physical personal care or lifting. State clearly in writing to the ward manager: "I love my relative, but I am physically/work-wise unable to provide personal care or safe transfers. They require a formal Pathway 1 Reablement or domiciliary care package."

2. "What happens if something goes wrong in the first 48 hours after they get home?"

Most NHS trusts operate a **72-Hour Post-Discharge Direct Line** Back to the Ward (printed on the Discharge Summary). Calling the ward directly within the first 48–72 hours often gets you instant advice from the doctor who treated them or fast-tracks a community nurse visit without waiting in GP queues.

3. "How do we get a pharmacy blister pack (Dosette box) so my parent doesn't mix up their pills?"

Hospitals usually send patients home with loose boxes for the first 14 days. On **Day 2 at home**, phone their regular high-street pharmacy and GP Practice Pharmacist and ask for a **Multi-Compartment Compliance Aid (Blister Pack) Assessment** before the hospital supply runs out.

4. "Why has my loved one become tearful, angry, or depressed since surviving a stroke?"

Up to 1 in 3 stroke survivors experience **Emotionalism (Pseudobulbar Affect)**—physical damage to the brain's emotion-control centres causing sudden crying or laughing without warning—alongside grief over lost independence. Acknowledge it gently without telling them to "cheer up," and mention it to the Stroke Community Team.

5. "They haven't opened their bowels in 4 days since coming home—is that an emergency?"

Hospital painkillers (especially codeine, co-codamol, or morphine) + reduced mobility almost always cause **severe post-discharge constipation**, which can trigger delirium, nausea, and urinary retention. Check if a PRN laxative (like Senna or Macrogol/Movicol) was included in their TTO bag, increase fluids, and call the GP/District Nurse by Day 3–4.

6. "What financial support can we claim immediately when care lasts longer than 6 weeks?"

Apply immediately for **Attendance Allowance** (for adults over State Pension age—worth £72.65 to £108.55/week tax-free, not means-tested) or **Personal Independence Payment (PIP)** (for under pension age). You do not need to wait to download the forms—getting the claim stamped early secures backdated payments.
