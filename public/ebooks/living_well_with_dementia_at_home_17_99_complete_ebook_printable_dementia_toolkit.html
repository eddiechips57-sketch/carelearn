Introduction • Stepping Into Their Reality

Why Arguing with Dementia Never Works (And What Does)

One of the most heartbreaking moments in family care happens around 4:30 PM. Your parent or spouse is standing in the hallway holding their coat, looking straight at the living room they have lived in for 40 years, and saying with tears in their eyes: "Please take me home. My mother is waiting for me."

Out of love, our natural instinct is to correct them with facts: "Dad, you ARE at home! You've lived here since 1984, and Grandma passed away 30 years ago." Within seconds, your loved one looks horrified, angry, or heartbroken—feeling either lied to by the person they trust most, or forced to relive bereavement all over again.

The #1 Rule of Dementia Care: Never force someone whose brain is broken to join your reality. Join theirs instead.

When they say "I want to go home," "home" is not a bricks-and-mortar address—it is a **feeling of safety, familiarity, and purpose** from an earlier decade of their life.

This playbook translates gold-standard clinical frameworks—including NICE Guideline NG97 (Dementia Assessment & Non-Cognitive Symptoms) ↗, Alzheimer's Society Sundowning Protocols ↗, and Dementia UK Admiral Nurse Communication Guidance ↗—into practical scripts and visual tools you can use tonight.

Chapter 1

The "Bookcase of Memory" & The 4-Step VERA Method

Think of your loved one's memories like a tall bookcase (a model developed by Social Care Institute for Excellence / SCIE ↗). Recent memories from today and this decade sit on the flimsy top shelves, while memories from their 20s and 30s (going to work, picking children up from school, living with their parents) are bolted onto the heavy bottom shelves. When dementia shakes the bookcase, the top shelves fall off—so to their brain, **it really is 1978, and they really do need to pick the children up.**

Clinical Diagram: The 4-Step VERA Communication Framework (Blackhall et al.)

Exact Script Swap: When They Ask for Work, School, or a Deceased Relative

❌ Reality Correction (Triggers Grief & Panic) ✅ VERA Validation Script (Restores Peace)
"Where is my mum?" → "Mum, Grandma died 25 years ago! You're 84!" "She's safe and everything is looked after today. You always loved her apple pie—tell me how she used to make it while I pop the kettle on."
"I have to pick the kids up from school!" → "Your kids are 50 years old!" "You are such a wonderful mum. Sarah has already picked the children up today so you can put your feet up in the warm."
"I'm late for my shift at the factory!" → "You retired 20 years ago!" "The manager phoned earlier to say you've got the afternoon off with full pay because you worked so hard this week."

Chapter 2

Beating "Sundowning": How to Calm the 4:00 PM to 8:00 PM Storm

Up to 66% of people living with dementia at home experience **Sundowning**—a surge of pacing, anxiety, suspicion, or shouting between late afternoon and early evening. According to Alzheimer's Society Clinical Guidance ↗, sundowning happens because the brain's internal body clock is damaged, cognitive fatigue peaks after a full day, and fading daylight casts scary shadows across the room.

Visual Protocol: The 4-Step Afternoon Sundowning Prevention Clock

📺 Ban the 5 PM TV News A person with moderate dementia cannot separate dramatic TV news or crime dramas from real events happening in their house. Switch to familiar music or nature shows after 3 PM.
🪞 Cover Reflections at Dusk Once indoor lights go on, uncovered dark windows look like mirrors. A person with dementia may not recognize their own older reflection and think a stranger is staring in at them.
🧺 Give a "5 PM Shift Job" 4:30 PM used to be rush hour—cooking tea or finishing work. Ask for their "help" folding a basket of tea towels or laying napkins on the table to satisfy that lifelong habit.

Chapter 3

Exit-Seeking & Wandering: Keeping Them Safe Without Feeling Locked In

When a person with dementia rattles the front door handle, seeing coats hanging on hooks and keys in a bowl acts as a visual trigger shouting "Time to leave!" Conversely, dementia changes 3D depth perception (University of Stirling DSDC Design Standards ↗)—meaning a dark doormat looks like a deep black hole in the floor, while a curtain pulled across a front door makes the exit disappear from view.

Environmental Blueprint: How Dementia Depth Perception Sees Doors & Floors

🚽 Check the Toilet First 50% of sudden "pacing toward the door" is actually someone looking for the bathroom who can't remember which white door it is. Stick **Toolkit #1 (Yellow Toilet Sign)** on the door today.
📍 GPS Sole / Keyring Tag Sew a discreet GPS tracker (Apple AirTag, Tile, or dementia GPS watch) into their favourite coat lining or shoe tongue, and complete **Toolkit #4 (Herbert Protocol)** on Page 13.
🌧️ The "Bad Weather" Bridge If they insist on leaving at night, walk to the window together: "It's pouring cold rain and the buses have stopped for tonight—stay in the warm guest room until morning."

Chapter 4

When They Refuse to Wash or Change Clothes: The "Towel-Under-Towel" Method

To someone with dementia, a cold tiled echoing bathroom, rushing overhead shower water, and someone pulling at their trousers feels terrifying and undignified. Never argue or say "You haven't washed in 4 days!" Instead, use the clinical **"Towel-Under-Towel" Warm Strip-Wash Technique** (endorsed by Dementia UK Admiral Nurses ↗):

Step-by-Step Visual Guide: The Zero-Exposure "Towel-Under-Towel" Wash

🚿 Ditch Overhead Spray Water spraying onto the face/head feels like drowning to someone with dementia. Use a handheld shower hose aimed only from the shoulders down, or no-rinse warm shampoo caps.
👔 The "Doctor's Visit" Invite A lifelong gentleman or lady who refuses to wash "for a carer" will often gladly freshen up if you say: "Let's have a quick freshen-up before the vicar/doctor pops round at 11."
🩲 Pull-Up Pants = Normal If they refuse incontinence pads, remove all normal underwear from their drawer and fill it only with soft pull-up continence pants—they look and feel just like normal underwear.

Chapter 5

Why Contrast Changes Everything: Plates, Toilet Seats & Cupboards

A famous study at Boston University discovered that simply switching from a **white dinner plate** to a **high-contrast bright red or yellow plate** increased food intake in people with Alzheimer's by **24%** and liquid intake by **84%**. Why? Because dementia damages contrast sensitivity—mashed potato, chicken, or milk on a white plate on a white tablecloth is virtually invisible to their eyes!

Visual Comparison: Low-Contrast (Invisible to Dementia) vs. High-Contrast (24% More Food Intake)

🚽 Fit a Coloured Toilet Seat A white toilet seat on a white bowl in a white bathroom causes missed aim and falls. A £15 navy blue or red toilet seat lets them spot the seat instantly.
🏷️ Label Key Kitchen Drawers Opening 6 cupboards to find a spoon is exhausting. Stick picture + word labels (from **Toolkit #1** or our Sign Pack) on the Tea/Mugs cupboard and Cutlery drawer.
🛋️ Contrast the Armchair If a beige armchair sits on a beige carpet, throw a bright contrasting blanket over the seat cushion so they know exactly where the seat edge is when sitting down.

Chapter 6

4 Real-World Playbooks: Accusations, Hallucinations & Repetition

Visual Protocol: How to Respond When Accused of "Stealing Their Purse, Money, or Keys"

Playbook 1: Repeating the Exact Same Question 20 Times Never say "I've already told you 10 times!"—that only makes them feel foolish and anxious, which makes them ask *more* often. Write the answer clearly on a small whiteboard placed beside their chair: **"Today is Tuesday. Sarah is visiting at 2:00 PM."** When they ask, smile and point gently to the board.
Playbook 2: Seeing People or Animals That Aren't There Common in Lewy body and Parkinson's dementia. If the vision isn't scaring them (e.g., seeing children or a cat), don't argue. If it *is* frightening them, turn on bright room lights to eliminate shadows and "shoo" the visitor out the door warmly: "Okay folks, visiting hours are over—off you go!"
Playbook 3: Hiding or Hoarding Tissue, Food, or Remotes Check their 3 "Safe Hiding Spots" every evening without scolding (usually inside pillowcases, tissue boxes, shoes, or behind sofa cushions). Leave a dedicated "rummage drawer" full of safe items they are allowed to sort and move around freely.
Playbook 4: Waking Up Dressed at 3:00 AM Ready for Breakfast Buy a large **Dementia Day/Night Digital Clock** that clearly displays **"3:00 AM — NOW IT IS NIGHT"** in bold letters. Keep bedroom curtains blackout-lined, leave a warm motion nightlight to the toilet, and guide them back with a warm decaf milky drink.

Chapter 7 • Validated Carer Questions

Dementia Care at Home: The Hard Questions Answered

Here are the six most urgent questions family carers and domiciliary support workers ask when supporting someone with dementia:

1. "Isn't using 'therapeutic fibs' (like saying their late mother is fine) dishonest or wrong?"

Clinical ethicists and Admiral Nurses call this **Emotional Truthfulness**. Telling an 85-year-old with Alzheimer's that their mother is dead forces them to experience raw, day-one bereavement grief repeatedly because their brain cannot retain the fact. Meeting them in their timeline is an act of deep compassion, not deception.

2. "My parent's dementia has worsened dramatically over just 48 hours—is this a sudden progression?"

**Almost never.** Dementia progresses over months and years, not overnight. A sudden 24–48 hour drop into severe confusion, aggression, or unsteadiness is almost always **Delirium** triggered by a **Urinary Tract Infection (UTI), chest infection, dehydration, or constipation**. Contact the GP or NHS 111 the same day for a urine dipstick test.

3. "Are we legally allowed to lock the front door if my spouse keeps trying to wander into traffic?"

Under the UK **Mental Capacity Act 2005**, you have a duty of care to act in the **Best Interests** of someone who lacks capacity to judge road safety. However, to prevent fire hazards, never hide keys where carers or family cannot reach them in 5 seconds—use a high/low thumb-turn lock, door chime alert, and camouflaged door curtain instead.

4. "How do we stop them from leaving the gas hob switched on or flooding the kitchen sink?"

Contact your energy supplier (via the free **Priority Services Register**) or a Gas Safe engineer to fit a **Lockable Gas Cooker Isolation Valve** (which looks like a simple tap you turn off when not cooking), and install a £8 ** Flood-Stopper Plug** in bathroom and kitchen sinks that automatically drains water before it overflows.

5. "Did you know families living with dementia qualify for a 25% to 100% Council Tax Discount?"

Many families miss out on thousands of pounds because councils rarely advertise the **Severe Mental Impairment (SMI) Council Tax Disregard**. Anyone with a medical diagnosis of dementia who receives Attendance Allowance or PIP is disregarded for Council Tax—saving 25% if they live with one adult carer, or 100% if they live alone.

6. "What happens if I fall ill or get taken to hospital—how will paramedics know I have someone with dementia at home?"

Register with your local Carer Support Service for a free **Carer's Emergency Card** to keep in your wallet/purse. If you are ever in an accident, emergency services call the 24-hour hub number on the card to activate your pre-agreed backup care plan immediately.
